The Case for Boring Tools

Every few months a new framework promises to make everything I already know how to do faster, cleaner, or more "delightful." Some of them are genuinely good. Most of them cost more than they save, once you count the migration, the retraining, and the six months of Stack Overflow answers that don't exist yet.

I used to treat my stack like a wardrobe — always looking for the next thing to try on. Somewhere around my third framework migration in two years, I noticed the actual work wasn't getting any better. Just newer.

Boring tools don't ask you to think about them. That's the whole point.

What "boring" actually means

Boring doesn't mean old, and it definitely doesn't mean bad. It means the rough edges are already known, documented, and probably already fixed by someone else. A boring tool has a community that's stopped arguing about the fundamentals and started arguing about the interesting problems.

When I pick a tool now, I ask one question: will I still be glad I chose this in three years, when the excitement has worn off and all that's left is the maintenance? If the honest answer is no, I keep looking — but I stop mistaking novelty for progress.

The tools I keep coming back to

Plain HTML where a framework isn't earning its keep. A relational database before anything fancier. A monospace font in my editor and nowhere else. None of it is exciting to write about, which might be the strongest argument in its favor.

Reading Code Like a Story

The first time I reviewed a pull request line by line, top to bottom, I caught every style violation and missed the fact that the whole feature solved the wrong problem. Since then I've reviewed code in a completely different order, and it's changed what I catch.

Now I start with the entry point — the button click, the API route, the cron job — and follow it the way a reader follows a plot. Who calls this? What did they want to happen? What actually happens three functions later?

Bugs hide in the implementation. Bad decisions hide in the plot.

Character, setting, plot

Every codebase has characters (the core objects), a setting (the constraints it operates under — latency, scale, who's allowed to call what), and a plot (the actual sequence of decisions a request goes through). Reading for plot first means I understand intent before I judge implementation.

It also means I ask better questions in review. Instead of "why did you name this tmp," I ask "why does this function need to know about billing at all." One of those questions changes a variable name. The other one changes an architecture.

A small practice

Before reviewing anything substantial now, I spend two minutes writing a one-sentence summary of what the change does, in plain language, before I look at a single diff. If I can't write that sentence, that's the first thing I flag.

Whitespace Is a Design Decision

Early in my career, a design director looked at a dashboard I'd built and asked why every panel was fighting for attention. I didn't have an answer, because I hadn't decided anything — I'd just filled the space that was there.

Whitespace isn't the absence of a decision. It's a decision that says: this thing next to it matters more than something else would have. Every pixel of margin is a small claim about hierarchy.

If everything is emphasized, nothing is.

The test I use now

Before adding density to a screen, I ask what I'm actually solving for. Sometimes the honest answer is "the client wants to see more on one screen," which is a real constraint — but it's different from "the user needs more on one screen," and the two call for different layouts entirely.

Generous spacing isn't a style, it's a tool for telling someone where to look first, second, and never. Tight spacing has its place too — a data table earns its density. A homepage rarely does.

One rule that's held up

Group by relationship, separate by difference, and make the gap between groups noticeably bigger than the gap within one. It sounds obvious written down. It's the thing I still get wrong first and fix second.

What Five Side Projects Taught Me

Only one of the five side projects I've shipped in the last four years has real, ongoing users. If I measured them purely by outcome, four of them were a waste of evenings. I don't think that's true, and here's why.

The habit tracker nobody used

My first project taught me how to ship something end to end, alone, without a team to catch my mistakes. That skill — not the app itself — is what I still use every day.

The recipe app I abandoned

I quit this one three weeks in, once I realized I didn't actually want to build it, I wanted to have built it. That's an expensive distinction to learn cheaply, and side projects are the cheapest place to learn it.

A side project's job isn't always to succeed. Sometimes it's to teach you what you don't want to build again.

The design system that became a job

Component Kit started as a weekend project to solve my own annoyance with a client's inconsistent buttons. Two years later, it's used in products I've never seen, and it's a meaningful line on my résumé. I didn't plan that. I just kept maintaining it after the annoyance was gone.

What I'd tell someone starting their first one

Pick something you'll want to open again in a week, not because it's ambitious, but because you're curious what happens next. Ambition fades. Curiosity, in my experience, is what actually ships things.
